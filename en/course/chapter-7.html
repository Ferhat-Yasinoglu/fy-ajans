Chapter 7 · The Summit

Skill isn't money — learn to convert it

For six chapters we built something: you got to know me, learned the request, connected flows, opened the workshop, made your system, became visible. Now the last and shortest stretch: where all of it turns into income. This chapter isn't sales technique; it's writing your offer, setting your price, running the first call and delivering the work. This is the seventh waypoint; what comes after is yours.

Reading time ~50 minutes · Prerequisite: Chapter 6

By the end of this chapter

  • You'll know how offer, price and pipeline work together
  • You'll reduce who you sell to into one sentence
  • You'll stop selling hours and write three packages
  • You'll set your first number with a method, not a guess
  • You'll be able to run the first call step by step
  • You'll protect yourself on contract, payment and delivery
  • You'll see how to turn one-off work into recurring revenue

    01Skill isn't money

    Over six chapters you picked up real skills. But there's a gap between skill and income, and talent doesn't close it. There are people doing excellent work who go hungry; people doing average work who live comfortably. The difference is usually one thing: one of them turned their work into an offer, the other didn't.

    This chapter is about that conversion. I'm not teaching persuasion tricks; persuading is the wrong target anyway. What I'm teaching is saying what you do in a way the other side understands, asking for a clear number in return, and keeping the promise you made. Sales looks hard mostly when those three are missing.

    An honesty note: this chapter is not a get-rich promise. Nowhere in this course did I say «you'll earn this much», and I won't here either. What I can say: someone whose offer is clear, whose price is considered and whose delivery is reliable consistently ends up better off than someone doing the same work with all three in a mess.

    📝Do this now: Write these two questions down and leave them blank: «What am I selling?» and «For how much?» Most people can't answer either clearly. By the end of this chapter both will be filled in.

    02Three words: offer, price, pipeline

    In every chapter three words opened the whole field. The last one is no different. These three are the three parts of an engine: miss one and it won't turn, have all three and it turns by itself.

    🔍Do this now: Which of the three is weakest for you? Be honest. Most readers will say «pipeline» but the real answer is usually «offer». Mark the weak one; you'll spend more time on it in this chapter.

    03Who are you selling to: narrow it

    «I provide services to everyone» sounds like a wide market but in practice means reaching nobody. Because a sentence addressed to everyone is a sentence in which nobody sees their own problem.

    The fear is «if I narrow, I'll lose work». In practice the opposite happens. A narrow description makes you a specialist; specialists are trusted more easily and paid better. And in a narrow field the second job is three times faster than the first — the «three times faster the second time» sentence from the lab chapter is exactly this.

    🎯Do this now: Apply the fourth step's pattern to your own work: «For ___ who ___, I build ___ in ___.» It can be long; it just has to be clear.

    04The anatomy of an offer

    An offer isn't a list of what you do. The other side isn't buying what you do, they're buying what they'll end up with. Five parts; all short.

    I'm stressing the second part in particular. If you don't write down what's outside the scope, the job grows without limit, you get worn out, and the client isn't happy either — because neither of you knew what was expected. The line «this price does not include…» is the line that protects the relationship.

    📦Do this now: Describe your most recent job to me and say: «Turn this into a one-page offer with these five parts; write the out-of-scope too.» Read what comes out — you're probably seeing your offer for the first time.

    05Don't sell hours: three packages

    Pricing by the hour has two problems. First, the faster you get the less you earn — so improving is punished. Second, the client doesn't want your hours but your result; talking hours makes you both look at the wrong thing. The fix: three packages priced by outcome.

    The most common mistake when writing packages is piling everything into the large one. The right way: the small package should be incomplete, not faulty. It should work on its own, but leave one thread open, so the client naturally wants the continuation.

    🏷️Do this now: Write your own three packages. Each with a name, a scope and three out-of-scope lines. We'll put numbers on them in the next section.

    06Finding your first number

    «How much should I ask for?» always comes out low when answered by guessing. Use a method; five steps and fifteen minutes.

    1. 1Find the floor: how many hours does this job take you, and what do you value your hour at? Multiply. That's your lower limit, not your price.
    2. 2Estimate the value: what does this job earn or save the client? How many hours a month, how many customers, how much loss? Produce a rough annual figure.
    3. 3Sit between the two: your price should be above the floor and clearly below the annual value. The client has to be able to see they come out ahead.
    4. 4Check the market once: look at three people doing the same work. The aim isn't to copy them but to know where the band is.
    5. 5Write the number down and say it out loud. If your voice shakes, either the number is wrong or you're not used to it yet. Getting used to it takes a few calls.

    A rule: start cheap with your first client, but don't start free. Free work leaves no sense of value on the other side and pulls you into an endless stream of requests. Even a symbolic number turns the relationship into a business relationship.

    🧮Do this now: Apply the five steps to your medium package. Then tell me: «I'm offering this job at this price, here's my reasoning — does it hold up, where is it weak?» Let's test the reasoning together.

    07Where the first client comes from

    The first client is almost never a cold stranger. There are five channels, and in practice the top two bring most of the first jobs.

    The top channel is most often missed out of embarrassment. «It's rude to message people I know about work.» What's actually a shame is being able to do a job well while nobody knows about it. The sentence can be simple: «I've started doing this — would it be useful to you or someone you know?»

    📇Do this now: Pull twenty names from your phone: people who trust you and roughly know what you do. Write to five of them today. Don't sell — just say what you do and ask.

    08The first call, step by step

    The call is what most people fear most; yet it's the easiest part to structure. Twenty minutes, five steps, a fixed order. The animation below shows the order — watch who's doing the talking.

    The third step matters more than it looks. When you say back what you understood in your own words, two things happen: if you misunderstood, it gets corrected; if you understood, the person thinks «this one gets me». Most of the sale finishes there; the rest is detail.

    Say the price on the call and repeat it in the offer. Avoiding it makes you look uncertain and forces a second call. After you say the number, stop talking — wait for the answer. That silence is uncomfortable, and that's exactly why it works.

    🎧Do this now: Write these five steps on a piece of paper and put it in front of you during the call. Looking at a sheet on your first three calls is nothing to be ashamed of; a structured conversation beats a memorised one.

    09Sending the offer

    The offer should go out within twenty-four hours of the call. Delay cools interest, and most lost work is lost in that gap. The content is already there: the five parts from 04.

    Format

    One page, plain text or a simple document. This isn't a design competition; it should be easy to read and easy to approve. The other side will read it on their phone.

    Language

    Use the words you heard on the call. If they said «reservation book», write «reservation book», not «data entry process». Someone who sees their own words feels understood.

    Validity

    The line «this offer is valid for two weeks» isn't a pressure tactic but an honesty: your prices and your calendar change. It also saves you from waiting on a decision forever.

    After sending, follow up: one short message three working days later. «Did you see the offer, any questions?» If there's no reply, one more a week later, then let it go. Two follow-ups is enough; the third damages the relationship.

    📨Do this now: Turn the offer we produced in 04 into a template, leaving the variables in square brackets. After your next call you'll fill it in in ten minutes.

    10No, and negotiation

    Not every offer gets accepted, and it shouldn't. If your acceptance rate is a hundred percent your price is too low. «No» isn't a failure, it's information — and often a «not now».

    Discount

    «Fine, I'll take twenty percent off.» You've done the same work for less; and the client learns your first number was inflated. They'll never believe another price of yours.

    Scope

    «With that budget we take this out and keep that.» The price drops because the work drops. Your reasoning stands, and the client knows what they're not getting.

    Also know that «expensive» usually isn't about price. «Expensive» generally means «I didn't understand the value». Instead of discounting at that moment, go back: what was this going to earn you? Do the value calculation from section six together. Half the time the number stays and the job happens.

    🤝Do this now: Decide in advance on two items that can come out of each of your three packages. Then you won't have to think during a negotiation; you'll have a ready list of concessions.

    11Contract and payment

    On small jobs a contract feels excessive — until it happens to you once. You don't need a complex document: the offer itself, with four lines added, becomes an agreement.

    1

    Deposit

    Part of the fee is taken up front — half is common. This isn't a sign of distrust, it's standard. A client who won't pay a deposit is often a client who'll struggle to pay at all; better to learn early.

    2

    Number of revisions

    «Two rounds of revisions included, further rounds charged separately.» Without this line the job never ends. Setting a limit reassures the client too: they know how many they have.

    3

    Who provides what

    Texts, images, account access — who sends them and when? Most projects are late not because you're slow but because the expected material never arrived. Write it down so the timeline depends on both of you.

    4

    Permission to use

    That one line from the showcase chapter: «may I use this work in my marketing?» Now it goes into the agreement. It's easier than asking afterwards, and most clients say yes.

    📄Do this now: Give me your offer template and say «add these four items and turn it into a simple agreement». I don't want long legal language; I want plain sentences both sides understand.

    12Delivery: give what you promised

    Winning the job is half; delivery is the other half. And an agency's long-term income comes almost entirely from this half, because referrals and repeat work are both born here. Three rules are enough.

    Keep them informed

    A short status message once a week: what's done, what's left, what I need from you. Two lines. A silent supplier worries people more than a late one.

    Show it early and unfinished

    Show the half-finished work. A surprise delivery is a bad idea: working four weeks in the wrong direction is far more expensive than hearing «that's not how I pictured it» in the first week.

    Make the handover easy

    Record a five-minute video at delivery explaining how to use it. For the systems you built in the lab chapter, that removes half the support messages.

    And one fact about delays: when you're going to be late, say so in advance. Someone who announces a delay on the delivery day loses trust; someone who says a week earlier «this will slip three days for this reason» usually loses nothing. The «breaking silently» rule from the agent chapter applies to human relationships too.

    📦Do this now: If you have a job in progress, send a two-line status message today. If not, write your weekly status template now for your first client — three items is enough.

    13Recurring revenue

    Living on one-off jobs means starting from zero every month. If your income is zero on the first of the month, what matters isn't how well you worked but how well you sold. Recurring revenue changes that ground.

    What gets sold monthly? The continuation of what you built: maintaining the flows, producing content, reporting, monthly improvements. The rule is this — a monthly package must contain real, visible work. A «support package» with no work in it gets cancelled in the second month. Send one page at month's end: what I did this month, what changed.

    🔁Do this now: Write the monthly part of your large package: which three jobs get done each month and what gets reported at month's end? Three items is enough, but make them real.

    14If you have your own business

    You've read this chapter so far as if you'd be selling services. But if you have your own business — a shop, a workshop, a café — the same chapters give the same result by another route. You're not looking for clients; you have them. What you need is to get more out of the same work.

    1

    Catch the interest you're losing

    Every unanswered message is a lost sale. The DM flow from the showcase chapter and the automatic greeting from the agent chapter pay for themselves in the first month in most small businesses — without finding a single new customer, just by not losing the ones who come.

    2

    Take your hours back

    If the flows from the agent and workshop chapters save you five hours a week, you spend those five hours either growing the business or on yourself. Both are income; the second gets talked about less.

    3

    Automate the return visit

    Getting a past customer to come back is many times cheaper than finding a new one. Your CRM knows this: a «came three months ago, never returned» list comes out monthly with one flow, and an automatic reminder goes.

    4

    Sell what you built

    If you've built a system that works in your own business, someone else in the same sector wants it. That's a second income line — and the easiest to sell, because the proof is your own business.

    🏪Do this now: Which of these four gives you the fastest result? Pick one and build it this week. Trying all four at once is the most common way of finishing none.

    15Capacity: one person's limit

    If it goes well a problem appears, and you should be ready for it: work starts arriving faster than your hours. There are three ways out; all three are legitimate but they have different consequences.

    Raise the price

    The simplest solution and the most postponed. If demand exceeds your hours, your price is low. Raise it twenty percent; some will leave, and with the rest you earn the same money in fewer hours.

    Automate more

    The first six chapters of this course exist for this. Turn the steps you repeat with every new client into a skill or a flow. By the third client, setup time is halved.

    Hire someone

    The last thing to consider. Hiring means managing, and managing is a new job. Exhaust the first two first — most one-person agencies reach a comfortable income with those alone.

    A warning: when your capacity is full, turning down new work feels hard and people say «I'll squeeze it in». If it doesn't fit, it doesn't fit. A badly delivered job costs more than one never taken — you lose both the money and your name.

    ⚖️Do this now: How many hours a week can you genuinely give to client work? Write it down. How many hours does your medium package take? Divide. That number is your monthly ceiling — you can't talk price without knowing it.

    16When to say no

    Saying no is hard when income is a worry; but the cost of saying yes to the wrong job is almost always higher. Four signs; if even one is there, stop and think.

    The result doesn't depend on you

    If the job's success depends on something you don't do — their team, their budget, their decision — the failure still gets written next to your name. Either write the boundary in or don't take it.

    Haggling before it starts

    A client who pulls the price down three times will argue about everything during the job too. The person who thinks they got it cheap is the person who asks for the most.

    Arriving in a rush

    «Needed tomorrow» jobs are either something someone else dropped or something planned badly from the start. Both are problems being handed to you. Charge a rush fee or pass.

    It doesn't sit right

    An unease you can't justify is usually justified. In a small business your attention is the most valuable asset; the wrong client eats three months of it.

    The polite form of no is the short one: «This job isn't right for me, but you could try this.» A long explanation invites debate. Refer them on if you can; a client you turned down and pointed in the right direction will still recommend you.

    🚪Do this now: Write your no sentence now and save it. Writing it in the moment is hard; a ready sentence makes a difficult moment easier.

    17Five common mistakes

    1 · Avoiding saying the price

    «Let me work it out and come back» is sometimes honest, mostly fear. If your packages are ready, the number is said on the call. Saying it and going quiet always beats avoiding it.

    2 · Not writing what's out of scope

    Anything unwritten counts as included. The job grows, the timeline stretches, you both end up unhappy. «These are not included» is the most valuable line in the agreement.

    3 · Starting without a deposit

    A significant share of unpaid jobs started in good faith end up half-finished. A deposit commits the other side too; insurance for you, a decision for them.

    4 · Going silent during delivery

    Someone who says nothing for two weeks is assumed to be working badly even when they're working well. Two lines a week is as valuable as the work itself.

    5 · Not asking for referrals

    A happy client won't mention you unprompted; it doesn't occur to them. Ask the moment the job ends: «if you're happy with this, do you know anyone with a similar problem?» This is the cheapest growth there is.

    🩹Do this now: Paste these five under your offer template as a checklist. Run through it in thirty seconds before every offer you send.

    18What everyone asks

    How do I win work with no references?

    Use proof instead of references. The system you built on yourself is proof: «I built this in my own business and got this result» is enough for the first three clients. Second, take the first job from someone you know — there, trust already stands in for references. After three jobs this question disappears.

    My voice shakes when I say my price.

    That's normal and it passes. Two practical helps: say the number out loud ten times, and do a rehearsal call with me — I'll play the client and raise the objections. By the third real call the shake is gone. And know this: a number with a reason behind it is easier to say than one without.

    The client said «let me think» and vanished.

    Most of the time that's a no, a polite one. What to do: put a validity period in the offer and send two follow-ups. After that, let it go. One more thing — if no «next step» was agreed at the end of the call, the chance of vanishing rises sharply; the fifth step in section eight was exactly for this.

    Should I say I use AI?

    If asked, yes; volunteering it isn't required. The client is buying a result; which tools you work with is your method. But two boundaries: don't lie when asked, and the responsibility for what you deliver is yours — «the AI wrote it that way» is not a valid excuse. The same honesty line as in the showcase chapter.

    How much will I earn after this course?

    I don't know, and nobody does — it depends on your sector, your city, how much you work and how many people you know. What I can say: someone who does the steps in this chapter usually reaches their first client within weeks, and steady income within months. Be suspicious of anyone who promises you a number.

    I finished the course, what's next?

    Go back to the start. That isn't a joke: on a second reading half the chapters land differently, because now you have a business to apply them to. And then there's the upkeep of everything you built: grow your CLAUDE.md, review your flows, write your four numbers weekly. A summit isn't an arrival, it's a place that needs maintaining.

    💬Do this now: If you're nervous before a call, write to me: «play the client, I'm selling this, ask hard questions.» Let's rehearse. You'll have heard most of the real objections in advance.

    20Test yourself

    Answer it yourself first, then open it.

    Offer, price, pipeline — which comes first?

    In that order. For someone without a clear offer and price, more interest just means more wasted calls. Most people work only on the third.

    Why narrow down?

    In a sentence addressed to everyone, nobody sees themselves. A narrow description makes you a specialist; specialists are trusted more easily, paid better, and the second job finishes three times faster.

    The five parts of an offer?

    Result, scope, timeline, price, next step. One page. The «not included» part of the scope matters as much as the «included».

    Why not price by the hour?

    The faster you get the less you earn — improving is punished. And the client wants your result, not your hours. The fix: three packages priced by outcome, the middle one the target.

    How do you find the first number?

    The floor (your hour × the time) is a lower limit, not a price. Estimate the annual value to the client; the price sits above the floor and clearly below that value. Check the market once, write the number, say it out loud.

    Where do most first clients come from?

    From people you know and past clients. The higher up, the more trust comes ready-made; the lower down, the more you build from zero. Content accumulates in the background; direct outreach has the lowest rate.

    The five steps of a first call?

    Listen, measure, mirror, propose, close. The client talks in the first half, you in the second. The third step — saying back what you understood — finishes most of the sale.

    What do you do when they say «expensive»?

    Not a discount — scope. «With that budget we take this out.» The price drops because the work drops. And «expensive» usually means «I didn't understand the value»; go back to the value calculation.

    The four lines of a contract?

    Deposit, number of revisions, who provides what, permission to use. Add those four to the offer and you have an agreement; no long legal language needed.

    What's the order when capacity fills?

    Raise the price first, automate more second, hire last. Hiring means managing and managing is a new job. Not taking a job that doesn't fit is cheaper than delivering it badly.

    This chapter in one sentence

    Reduce who you sell what to into one sentence, write three packages and set the number with a method; find your first client among people you know, listen first and propose second on the call, send the offer within a day; take a deposit, write the out-of-scope, keep them informed during delivery — and turn one-off work into a monthly ground.
